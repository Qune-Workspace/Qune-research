## 1. What is robust

**Trend (time-series momentum) across asset classes.** The best-documented systematic effect in liquid futures and, by extension, in the ETFs that track them. Reported: Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen [MOP12] find that the past 12-month excess return of each of 58 futures contracts predicts its next-month return, in every asset class including government bonds, with a diversified long/short portfolio that performs best in extreme markets. Reported: Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen [HOP17] extend the evidence to 1880–2016 and find the effect positive in every decade, with its largest gains in the biggest equity bear markets. Reported: Georgopoulou and Wang [GW17] find the same across equity index and commodity futures with a different sample and construction, and Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen [AMP13] find cross-sectional value and momentum in every asset class, so the effect is not a futures artefact. Industry: 2022, when stocks and bonds fell together, is the clearest out-of-sample episode since the 2012 paper: the SG Trend Index returned 27.4% in 2022 against 20.9% in 2008 [CA25], and the twenty largest CTAs tracked by Société Générale booked a record 20% gain for the year [HN23]. Reported: Hamill, Rattray and van Hemert [HRvH16] find trend following positive in most equity and bond crises of the past century, with slow bond sell-offs the harder case, which matters for a rates book. Inferred: the effect is robust at the portfolio level across many assets; the evidence for timing a *single* instrument with it is far weaker (section 2).

**Carry, across asset classes.** Reported: Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt [KMPV18] define carry as the return if prices do not change (for bonds: the yield spread over the funding rate plus roll-down) and find it predicts returns in equities, bonds, commodities, currencies and credit, with a diversified carry portfolio that is largely uncorrelated with trend. Reported: Brooks, Palhares and Richardson [BPR18] find value, momentum, carry and defensive styles priced in sovereign and corporate bonds. Inferred: carry is the natural complement to trend in a rates book because it is a different signal (level of yields and slope) rather than a different look-back of the same prices.

**Volatility targeting of risk assets.** Reported: Moreira and Muir [MM17] find that scaling exposure by the inverse of recent realised variance raises Sharpe ratios and alphas for the market and several factors. Reported: Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison and van Hemert [HHKRSV18] find the gain concentrated in equities and credit, where volatility clusters and returns fall when volatility rises, and little or none in bonds, currencies and commodities. Reported: Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan [COWY20] find that the in-sample alphas of volatility-managed portfolios mostly do not survive as real-time, out-of-sample gains for a mean-variance investor, except for the market factor. Inferred: vol targeting is a sound risk-control device for a cross-asset book and a doubtful source of return on its own; for a Treasury-only rule such as Study 003 it should not be expected to add return, and we will not claim it does.

**Multiple-testing discipline.** Reported: Harvey, Liu and Zhu [HLZ16] count the published factor tests and argue a new effect needs a t-statistic above about 3.0, not 2.0. Reported: Bailey and López de Prado [BLdP14] give the deflated Sharpe ratio, a Sharpe ratio corrected for the number of trials and non-normal returns; Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu [BBLZ16] give the probability of backtest overfitting from combinatorial splits. Reported: White [W00] and Hansen [H05] give the reality check and the test for superior predictive ability for comparing many strategies against a benchmark; Ledoit and Wolf [LW08] give a robust test for the difference of two Sharpe ratios, and Politis and Romano [PR94] the stationary bootstrap those tests rest on. Observed: this programme already uses a trial ledger and the deflated Sharpe ratio at the logged N (Study 003: DSR 0.95 at N = 3, in-sample); the SPA test and the Ledoit-Wolf test are not yet implemented and belong in the shared library.

## 2. What has decayed, or was never there

**Published anomalies decay.** Reported: McLean and Pontiff [MP16] find that the average published cross-sectional predictor's return falls about 26% out of sample and about 58% after publication, consistent with investors trading on it. Reported: Calluzzo, Moneta and Topaloglu [CMT19] find institutions trade on anomalies after publication, which is the mechanism. Inferred: any signal taken from a paper should be expected to deliver well under half of its published return; our pre-registered benchmarks should be set against that, not against the paper's table.

**Single-instrument time-series momentum is weaker than it looks.** Reported: Huang, Li, Wang and Zhou [HLWZ20] find that the pooled time-series predictability behind [MOP12] is not significant asset by asset, and that the trading strategy's profit is largely matched by one that uses no predictability at all. Reported: Kim, Tse and Wald [KTW16] find that much of time-series momentum's reported performance comes from volatility scaling and a net long bias rather than from the sign of the past return. Reported: Goyal and Jegadeesh [GJ18] find that the difference between time-series and cross-sectional momentum is a time-varying net long position in the market. Observed: Study 003 is one instance of exactly this: 12-month momentum on IEF alone trailed buy-and-hold in-sample (Sharpe 0.45 vs 0.55 over 185 months, t = 0.37) and its out-of-sample gain came from a single exit in 2021 (one episode, t = 1.31). Inferred: a single-instrument trend rule is a low-power test of an effect that is diversified across many assets; this is the main reason Study 004 is a portfolio.

**Bond return predictability from the yield curve: strong in sample, little out of sample.** Reported: Campbell and Shiller [CS91] and Cochrane and Piazzesi [CP05] show that yield spreads and a combination of forward rates predict excess bond returns in sample, with R² around 0.3 to 0.4 for the forward-rate factor. Reported: Thornton and Valente [TV12] find that the same predictors give no out-of-sample economic value to an investor over the expectations-hypothesis benchmark; Ludvigson and Ng [LN09] find macro factors add in-sample predictability; Bianchi, Büchner and Tamoni [BBT21] find machine-learning combinations of yields and macro data add out-of-sample predictability, which an investor should treat as a many-trials result. Reported: Haddad and Sraer [HS20] find bank exposure to interest-rate risk predicts bond returns, a non-yield-curve channel. Observed: Studies 001 and 002, which would have tested a 10-year minus 3-month spread switch on IEF, halted at data validation (a synthetic pre-2002 bond series failed its fidelity limits), so this programme has no evidence of its own either way. Inferred: the yield-curve switch is a low-expected-value question in ETF-era data; it stays on the list (section 5, question 5) only as a negative-result study, with the trial count and the benchmark set accordingly.

**Trend in the 2010s.** Industry: an allocator's 2025 note, written to ask whether the strategy is structurally broken after its post-2009 stretch, counts 16 SG Trend Index drawdowns of more than 10% since 2000, roughly one every 18–24 months [CA25]; a trend manager's own 2019 study argues the weak decade was an environment effect rather than crowding, from the absence of an alpha-decay gradient with signal lag [BK19], which is an interested source. Reported: [HOP17] covers the decade and finds the century-long effect still positive. Inferred: a decade of flat performance is inside the historical range of the effect and is not evidence that it is gone; it is evidence that a trend book needs a long horizon and that a 2003–2018 in-sample window understates its drawdowns.

## 3. What is crowded

**Trend.** Industry: the slow trend signal is the product of an entire managed-futures industry with its own benchmark indices [CA25, HN23], and the largest allocators buy it as a plain, low-cost diversifier rather than as a proprietary edge [HN23]. Industry: [BK19] finds no capacity footprint in liquid markets, from an interested source. Inferred: trend is crowded in the sense that the slow 12-month signal is universally known, so its forward return should be set at a fraction of the published one; a long-only or long/flat ETF version removes the short side and most of the capacity question, at the cost of giving up the short-side crisis returns.

**Carry.** Reported: Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen [BNP08] document that currency carry trades are crowded and unwind in crashes, with returns that are negatively skewed. Reported: [KMPV18] find bond carry less crash-prone than currency carry in their sample. Inferred: a bond carry signal on IEF, TLT and SHY is the least crowded of the carry family that our approved symbols can reach; currency carry through ETFs is both crowded and badly proxied.

**Cross-sectional equity momentum.** Reported: Daniel and Moskowitz [DM16] document momentum crashes in rebounds after bear markets. Inferred: out of scope for this programme's approved universe and listed only because its crash behaviour is the reason every momentum study here uses a drawdown table, not just a Sharpe ratio.

## 4. What our own three studies add

Observed, from the committed results (root README "Results at a glance", each study's README):

- A synthetic pre-ETF Treasury series cannot be built to a ±0.40% p.a. level and 1.5% p.a. tracking-error standard from constant-maturity yields alone; the second attempt came within 0.01% on level and failed on tracking error (2.02%). Any rates question we ask is therefore answered on actual ETF history from 2002–2003, which is three rate cycles, one zero-bound decade and one inflation shock: enough for a portfolio test, thin for a single-instrument one.
- Twelve-month momentum on IEF alone has no in-sample edge over holding IEF (2003–2018) and one good out-of-sample episode (sitting out 2022). That episode is exactly the period when the stock-bond correlation turned positive [CPV20, I03], which is the origin of question 3 below.
- The process holds: pre-registration before any return, a trial ledger, one touch of the out-of-sample window, independent recomputation of every published number (001: 69 of 69; 002: 156 of 157; 003: 171 of 171). The next studies inherit it unchanged.

## 5. The next five questions, ranked

Ranking criterion: strength of the published evidence after the decay discount, independence from what we have already tested, reach with the approved or delegated liquid-ETF universe, and what a negative result would still teach. Each question will be pre-registered before any return is computed; the pre-registration, not this ranking, is the binding document.

1. **Cross-asset trend with volatility targeting, long-only and long/flat (Study 004, flagship).** Universe about 12–20 liquid ETFs across equities, rates, credit, real assets and currencies; 12-month (and a pre-registered second look-back) time-series signal; ex-ante volatility target with position caps; per-ETF cost model; the volatility estimator and the trading rule chosen before any return, with the turnover consequences [BK12] reported. Why first: the strongest evidence [MOP12, HOP17, GW17] and the clearest out-of-sample episode (2022). What it must do that the literature's critics demand: separate the trend signal from the long bias and from volatility scaling by pre-registering the vol-scaled long-only portfolio as the benchmark [KTW16, HLWZ20, GJ18], report per-asset attribution, crisis tables (2008, 2020, 2022), correlation to SPY and IEF, a breakeven cost curve, and the deflated Sharpe ratio at the logged N. Downside case: a long-only ETF version of a long/short futures effect, over 2003–2018, may show no gain over the vol-scaled benchmark; that is a publishable negative result on the single question that matters most for the programme.
2. **Bond carry, and carry plus trend, in Treasuries (SHY, IEF, TLT vs BIL).** Carry = yield over bills plus roll-down, computed from the constant-maturity curve, which is public and needs no synthetic series. Why second: a different signal from Study 003 on the same instruments [KMPV18, BPR18]; the combination with trend is the standard rates book [BGHLS15]; a term-premium estimate from a published affine model [ACM13] is the natural control, reported but not traded. Downside case: carry on duration is mostly the term premium, which the ETF era paid unevenly; the study must report the signal's return separately from the long-duration exposure, with the exposure-matched benchmark Study 003 already uses.
3. **Trend exits as duration-risk control, conditional on the stock-bond correlation regime (Study 005 candidate, from our own finding).** Hypothesis from Study 003's one out-of-sample episode: a trend exit from duration adds value when the stock-bond correlation is positive (inflation-driven regimes) and costs when it is negative (demand-driven regimes), following the mechanism in [CPV20] and the long history in [I03]. Why third: it is the first question that comes from our own evidence rather than a paper, and the mechanism is grounded; why not higher: one episode is one data point, and the regime variable has to be defined ex ante from trailing data without a look-ahead. Downside case: the regime indicator is slow and the rule trades rarely, so the trial count stays small and the test is low-powered; the pre-registration must say what the minimum detectable effect is.
4. **Volatility-managed versus unmanaged exposure on the approved ETFs, with real costs.** The direct real-time test of [MM17] against [COWY20, HHKRSV18] on SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, IEF and TLT, with turnover and cost reported, which is what an allocator needs to know before any vol-targeted product. Why fourth: it is largely a by-product of Study 004's attribution and can be reported there; it becomes its own study only if Study 004's vol-scaled benchmark behaves differently from the published results.
5. **The yield-curve duration switch, re-asked as a negative-result study on ETF history only.** The question Studies 001 and 002 never reached, run on 2003–2018 IEF data without a synthetic series, with the benchmark set at the out-of-sample findings of [TV12]: the expected answer is "no economic value", and the study would be designed to measure how close to zero, with the trial count fixed at one. Why last: low expected value, and the ETF era contains too few inversions for a spread rule to switch often; it is kept because a clean negative result closes the question the programme opened with.

Not on the list, and why: currency trend and carry through ETFs (UUP, FXE, FXY) are poorly proxied and, for carry, crowded and crash-prone [BNP08, MSSS12, LRV11]; cross-sectional equity momentum needs single stocks, which are outside the approved universe [DM16]; machine-learning bond-return forecasts [BBT21] are a many-trials exercise that this programme's trial ledger would price at a deflated Sharpe near zero.

## 6. What the shared library needs before Study 004 runs

From sections 1 and 2: a vectorised backtest engine with an event check; a cost model with per-ETF spreads; Sharpe with heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent standard errors; the deflated Sharpe ratio and minimum track-record length [BLdP14]; the Ledoit-Wolf Sharpe difference test [LW08]; the stationary bootstrap [PR94]; the SPA test [H05] for the look-back and universe variants that Study 004 pre-registers; an ex-ante covariance estimate (exponentially weighted, shrunk) for the volatility target. Existing studies keep their frozen code.
